The Fear Circuit: A Guided Diagram

Three brain structures do most of the work in fear, and they act as a connected circuit rather than isolated parts. Click each node to learn what it does, then use what you find to answer the question underneath.

This is a deliberately simplified schematic, not an anatomical map. The real fear circuitry involves more structures and connections than are shown here (the reading names the hypothalamus and other regions too), and the boxes are placed for clarity, not to mirror where these structures physically sit. Aim to own the roles and how they interact rather than to memorize an anatomy.

Put the circuit together

You've opened all four structures. Now use them on a question.

Write first, then compare: A client says, “I know my flight is safe, so why does my body still panic on the runway?” In two or three sentences, answer using the circuit. Name which structure is doing what, and say why knowing a fact doesn't automatically switch the fear off.
A model explanation

The amygdala (the alarm) learned to treat flying as danger, and it fires fast, before the slower, thinking parts of the brain can weigh in. That speed is why the fear arrives even when the prefrontal cortex (the brake) holds accurate knowledge that flying is safe. The alarm route doesn't wait for the reasoning route, and it doesn't automatically update just because you looked up a statistic. Knowing something intellectually and feeling it in the body run on partly different systems, which is the gap the client describes as “I know I'm safe, but I don't feel safe.” The alarm changes mainly through repeated lived experience of safety rather than through facts alone, which is why exposure works by doing rather than only knowing.

This is a self-check, not a graded item. There is no single correct wording. Compare whether your explanation used the alarm's speed and the gap between knowing and feeling, and revise your mental model where it differs.
